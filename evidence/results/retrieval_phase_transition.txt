RECALL IS A PHASE TRANSITION, AND ONE-SHOT READING HIDES IT
===========================================================

THE COMPLAINT THAT PRODUCED THIS. Every result this session moved recall by percentages -- better
binding algebra, orthogonal axes, transport off, selective writes. The objection was that
percentage gains mean the axis itself is wrong. It was.

THE REFRAME. All of those changes improve the signal-to-noise of a SINGLE LINEAR READ. The state
is a superposition; one linear operator extracts signal plus crosstalk; better algebra shrinks the
crosstalk and never makes the read a decision. Attention is not better algebra: it does O(n) work
at read time, comparing the query against every stored item. Our recurrence does O(1) work at read
time no matter how good the binding is. That is a difference of computational class, not of
quality. (Jelassi et al. 2024 prove a separation of this shape for copying.)

Biology is on the O(n) side. Hippocampal CA3 retrieval is recurrent attractor dynamics -- pattern
completion by iterating to a fixed point (Hopfield 1982) -- not a projection. Ramsauer et al. 2020
showed the modern Hopfield update IS softmax attention.

So: RETRIEVAL FROM A SUPERPOSITION IS AN INFERENCE PROBLEM AND WE HAVE BEEN TREATING IT AS A
PROJECTION. Every number measured before this file is the accuracy of one step of an iteration
that should be run to convergence.

TEST 1 -- one-shot vs explain-away. HRR store h = sum_i k_i (*) v_i, query k_q. All keys are known
  (they appeared in the sequence), so a reader MAY decode the other pairs and subtract them. That
  is the O(n) read work attention does and the recurrence does not. 300 trials.

      d=128         n=2     n=4     n=8    n=16    n=32    n=64
      one-shot    1.000   1.000   0.983   0.770   0.447   0.183
      iterative   1.000   1.000   1.000   0.977   0.233   0.023

  Above threshold iteration is essentially EXACT -- 0.977 at n=16 where one-shot manages 0.770.
  Below threshold it is CATASTROPHIC, and worse than one-shot: a hard cleanup onto a wrong estimate
  injects a confidently wrong item, which corrupts the residual for every later step. Extra
  iterations change nothing (iters 1, 2 and 4 are identical) -- greedy matching pursuit converges
  immediately, correctly or wrongly.

THE FINDING -- FIRST HEADLINE WAS WRONG, CORRECTED HERE
  I first wrote: "the iterative boundary sharpens with state size while the one-shot curve never
  does, therefore the memory has a sharp capacity ~ d/8 and one-shot reading SMEARS it into a
  slope; the graceful-degradation curves in the literature are an artefact of the readout."

  The table above refutes that, and I did not check it before committing. Read n* at the 0.5 level
  for BOTH decoders, not just the iterative one:

      d=512    one-shot crosses 0.5 near n=96     iterative crosses 0.5 near n=70
      d=128    one-shot near n=32                 iterative near n=26
      d=32     one-shot near n=12                 iterative near n=10

  Greedy iteration has a LOWER median capacity than one-shot at every d. It is better near accuracy
  1.0 and worse in the tail. So no single "true capacity" is being hidden, and d/8 is a property of
  greedy matching pursuit, not of the memory -- which the n*/d column already hinted at by drifting
  0.25 -> 0.125 rather than holding.

  CORRECTED STATEMENT: the memory does not have one capacity. EACH DECODER HAS A THRESHOLD.
  Greedy iterative decoding is near-exact up to a boundary and catastrophic past it; one-shot
  decoding degrades gradually and overtakes it beyond that boundary. Which decoder is better
  depends entirely on the accuracy you demand -- and for a language model the demand is
  near-exactness, which is the regime where iteration wins.

  "One-shot never sharpens" is also wrong: d=32 one-shot falls 0.92 -> 0.21 over an 8x range in n,
  d=512 falls 0.96 -> 0.28 over 4x, so it steepens too, just slowly. Five values of d cannot
  distinguish "never" from "logarithmically". A max-of-n-Gaussians argument puts the one-shot
  threshold at n ~ d/log n, which is a threshold as well.

WHAT THE LITERATURE ALREADY SAYS -- and my claim about it was a straw man
  The theory is ALREADY threshold-shaped. Arora et al. (Zoology) prove Omega(N) state dimension for
  N-pair MQAR; Jelassi et al. 2024 is a separation theorem for copying. Both are cliffs, and
  arora2023zoology is ALREADY IN OUR OWN refs.bib. The smooth empirical curves in that literature
  are curves of TRAINED models, which are one-shot linear readers -- so a slope is exactly what the
  theory predicts you should measure from them. Consistent, not a misreading.

  Withdrawn: "an instrument too blunt to show a cliff rather than a hill", "the field's axis". This
  is the second time this session I have built a "the field missed it" argument against a paper
  already sitting in our bibliography, after krohn1965.

  What survives: trained fixed-state models are one-shot linear readers, so their recall curves are
  slopes, while the underlying bound is a threshold. That is worth stating plainly and is not a
  criticism of anyone.

WHAT REMAINS GENUINELY OPEN
  The class difference is real and is Jelassi's result, not ours: an O(1) LINEAR PROJECTION versus
  a NONLINEAR SELECTION over an item axis. The open question -- and the only one here that is not
  percentage-shaped -- is whether a FIXED-SIZE state can host nonlinear selection at read time.
  Classical Hopfield stores 0.14*d patterns in a d x d matrix WITH iteration; the same matrix read
  linearly (S q, which is DeltaNet) gets less. Whether that difference is a step or a slope is the
  decisive experiment, and iterations correspond to depth at the query position -- which our idle
  second layer never had a selection nonlinearity to run.

WHAT THIS CHANGES FOR SARYU
  - The binding-algebra results (binding_vs_tracking.txt) are not wrong, they are SUB-THRESHOLD
    engineering. They raise SNR inside a regime whose ceiling is set by n*/d.
  - Stop reporting recall as an accuracy at one n. Report the THRESHOLD n*, and whether a design
    change moves it. A change that lifts accuracy at fixed n without moving n* is noise on the
    wrong axis.
  - The honest framing for the paper: a fixed-size recurrent state is a compressed measurement of
    the past, retrieval from it is sparse recovery, and it therefore has a capacity threshold, not
    a capacity slope.

OPEN / NOT CLAIMED
  - The reader here is GREEDY matching pursuit with hard cleanup. A different decoder (convex
    relaxation, soft/annealed cleanup, resonator-style alternating projection) may put the
    threshold elsewhere. The claim is that a threshold EXISTS and sharpens with d, not that d/8 is
    the optimal decoder's boundary.
  - The reader is also an ORACLE: it is handed every key. A trained model is not. So n* here is a
    CEILING on what any fixed-state model with this scheme can reach, not a prediction of what one
    will reach.
  - n*/d drifts down with d (0.25, 0.25, 0.188, 0.125, 0.125), so the boundary is sublinear in d
    rather than a fixed fraction. Not fitted; more d values needed before naming a law.
  - All simulation. Nothing trained.
